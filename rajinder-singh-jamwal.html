A life of service · Jammu and Kashmir State Forces

Brigadier Rajinder Singh Jamwal

The Stand That Bought Time

14 June 1899 – Night of 26–27 October 1947 · Maha Vir Chakra (posthumous)

A historical account of the defence of the approaches to Srinagar during October 1947. Sources and further reading appear at the end.

Barely two months had passed since India celebrated its hard-won independence. While a young nation looked towards its future with hope, Brigadier Rajinder Singh and his men stood on the front line, fighting to defend the sovereignty of Jammu and Kashmir.

Early life and family

Brigadier Rajinder Singh Jamwal was born on 14 June 1899 in Bagoona, a village in the Jammu region, now known as Rajinderpura in Samba district, Jammu and Kashmir. He belonged to a Jamwal Dogra Rajput family with a tradition of military service.

His father, Subedar Lakha Singh, served in the Jammu and Kashmir State Forces. His grandfather was Hamir Singh, while General Baj Singh was an ancestor of the family who served under Maharaja Gulab Singh. After losing his father at an early age, Rajinder Singh was brought up by his paternal uncle, Lieutenant Colonel Govind Singh.

He studied at Prince of Wales College in Jammu, graduating in 1921. On 14 June that year, his twenty-second birthday, he was commissioned into the Jammu and Kashmir State Forces as a Second Lieutenant. He rose to the rank of brigadier in 1942 and, in September 1947, assumed the position of Chief of Staff of the Jammu and Kashmir State Forces.

Biographical details are drawn from the secondary profiles listed in the sources below. Family relationships are stated distinctly: General Baj Singh was an ancestor, Hamir Singh was his grandfather, Lakha Singh was his father, and Lieutenant Colonel Govind Singh was his uncle and guardian. [4] [5]

A State Under Threat

In October 1947, the security situation across Jammu and Kashmir deteriorated rapidly. The State Forces were dispersed across a long frontier, often in small detachments with limited arms, ammunition, supplies and communications.

On 22 October, armed tribal forces advanced into Jammu and Kashmir from the direction of Pakistan. Muzaffarabad and Domel were among the early locations overrun. Elements of the 4th Jammu and Kashmir Infantry mutinied, and the advancing forces exploited the resulting breakdown in the defence. The loss of the Kohala–Domel garrison exposed the route towards Srinagar. [1] [2]

At Badami Bagh Cantonment in Srinagar, 1,850 officers and men were reportedly stationed when the invasion began. Of these, approximately 500 Poonchie Muslim personnel were considered operationally unreliable following mutinies among elements of the Jammu and Kashmir State Forces. Brigadier Rajinder Singh did not deploy these personnel, leaving him to organise the relief force from the remaining personnel available under the circumstances.

The relief column

With no substantial reserve immediately available in Srinagar, Rajinder Singh organised a relief force from the personnel and equipment that could be mobilised. The force comprised a strong company, a section of medium machine guns and a detachment of 3-inch mortars. Captains Prithi Singh and Khazan Singh, and Lieutenant Nasib Singh, were placed under his command. [1]

The initial column is reported to have numbered approximately 150 men. This figure refers to the force sent towards Uri, not the total strength of Badami Bagh Cantonment or the reinforcements that arrived later. [1] [2]

Rajinder Singh left Srinagar on the evening of 22 October. The journey was slowed by difficult roads, rain, a shortage of petrol and the limitations of the available transport. Brahma Singh's account places the column's arrival at Uri around midnight, while Honourpoint gives approximately 2 a.m. on 23 October. The precise arrival time remains uncertain. [1] [2]

The first engagement at Garhi

Uri was selected as the principal defensive position. After organising the defences, Rajinder Singh moved forward with the major part of his force to make contact with the advancing enemy.

The first clash occurred near Garhi on the morning of 23 October. The small relief force encountered an opponent superior in numbers and equipment. During the engagement, Subedar Duni Chand, the leading platoon commander, was killed, and several other soldiers were wounded, including Captain Prithi Singh.

Rajinder Singh withdrew towards Hattian and established another delaying position. The engagement made clear that the advancing force was stronger than initially anticipated. To continue resisting, the defenders needed reinforcements and additional supporting weapons. Rajinder Singh communicated his requirements to Army Headquarters, including the dispatch of additional personnel, supporting weapons and other State Forces units towards Uri. [1]

Holding Uri

By nightfall on 23 October, Rajinder Singh's force had returned to its main position at Uri. Reinforcements arrived under Captain Jawala Singh. The additional force included an infantry platoon, a section of medium machine guns and a section of 3-inch mortars. Captain Jawala Singh also carried a written order from Maharaja Hari Singh directing Rajinder Singh to hold Uri at all costs. [1] [2]

On 24 October, the bridge over the Uri Nala was prepared for demolition. According to Brahma Singh's account, the defenders delayed destroying it so that refugees could cross. The bridge was then demolished as the enemy approached. The advancing force launched a frontal attack, which was repelled by machine-gun and mortar fire.

It subsequently attempted to outflank the defenders by moving across the Jhelum and around their positions. Facing the risk of being bypassed, Rajinder Singh ordered a withdrawal towards Mahura, where the defence could continue from another position. The withdrawal was a response to the danger that remaining at Uri would allow the enemy to move around the defenders and advance towards Srinagar. [1]

The defence of Mahura and Buniyar

The withdrawing column reached Mahura late on 24 October. At dawn on 25 October, the enemy attacked the hastily prepared defensive positions. The defenders repelled the assault, but the advancing force again attempted to move around their flanks and threaten their rear.

Rajinder Singh ordered Captain Jawala Singh and Lieutenant Nasib Singh to destroy two footbridges across the Jhelum near Buniyar. The demolitions were carried out, although some enemy troops had already crossed. Further attacks and outflanking movements eventually made the Mahura position untenable. The defenders withdrew towards Buniyar, where fighting continued on 26 October. [1]

The force again repelled attacks, but its own casualties and the continuing threat of encirclement made another withdrawal necessary. Rajinder Singh intended to move towards Seri, near the approaches to Baramulla, and establish the next defensive position. Across these engagements, the force repeatedly had to resist, assess the enemy's movements and withdraw before its positions could be cut off. [1]

The final action

The withdrawal from Buniyar began around midnight. According to Brahma Singh's account, the enemy had established a roadblock behind the defenders. When the vehicles reached it, they came under fire. The driver of the vehicle carrying Rajinder Singh was killed. The brigadier took the wheel himself, but was then shot in the leg and could no longer drive. The vehicle had to be abandoned. [1]

Brahma Singh attributes the subsequent account to Captain Khazan Singh, who was travelling in the same vehicle. According to this testimony as recounted by the author, Khazan Singh and his men offered to carry the wounded brigadier. Rajinder Singh recognised that doing so could hinder the withdrawal and directed them to continue towards the next defensive position. The men moved away from the vehicle under fire. A later vehicle passed through the roadblock without knowing that Rajinder Singh had been left behind. His surviving companions did not see him again. [1]

The precise circumstances of his final moments are known through later accounts, including the testimony attributed to Captain Khazan Singh. These details should be understood as reported testimony rather than a verbatim record of Rajinder Singh's own words.

A sacrifice remembered

The events of 22–27 October 1947 unfolded during a decisive period for Jammu and Kashmir. Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October, and the first Indian Army troops arrived in Srinagar on the morning of 27 October. [2] [5]

Rajinder Singh's force resisted the advance through successive engagements during the critical days before those troops arrived. The published Maha Vir Chakra citation recognises the delaying action undertaken by Rajinder Singh and the men under his command during this period. [2]

Brigadier Rajinder Singh was posthumously awarded the Maha Vir Chakra, India's second-highest wartime gallantry award. His contribution is remembered for the responsibility he accepted, the difficult decisions he made under pressure, and the resistance maintained by the men under his command. [2]

Remembering the men alongside him

The story of Brigadier Rajinder Singh is also the story of the soldiers who fought at Garhi, Uri, Mahura and Buniyar; the men who operated the machine guns and mortars; those who carried the wounded; and those whose names are less familiar today.

Their contribution should not be reduced to a footnote to their commander's sacrifice. The defence was carried out by a force whose strength was limited, whose positions were repeatedly threatened, and whose men continued to resist through successive engagements.

Born from respect. Built on remembrance.

At Veer Smriti, we remember Brigadier Rajinder Singh Jamwal not only for the circumstances of his final action, but also for the responsibility he carried and for every soldier who stood beside him.
